AI Foundations

A chatbot answering your question usually isn't looking anything up. It's predicting the next word, then the next one, really fast. Once you see how that works, it makes sense that chatbots sometimes invent things and sound totally sure about it.

How a chatbot writes

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot all run on large language models, or LLMs. An LLM is trained on a massive amount of text, including websites, books, and code. From all that reading, it learns which words tend to follow which.

When you send it a question, it doesn't pull up a stored answer. It reads the whole conversation so far, gives every possible next piece of text a score, picks one, adds it to the end, and repeats. A paragraph-long answer is hundreds of those tiny predictions in a row.

Tokens are the pieces

Those pieces are called tokens. A token can be a whole word, a chunk of a word, a word with the space in front of it, or a punctuation mark. Common words usually get one token each, and longer or rarer words get split up. OpenAI's rule of thumb is that one token is about four characters of English, so 100 tokens comes out to roughly 75 words.

Temperature: playing it safe or taking chances

Most AI tools have a setting called temperature. At a low temperature, the model almost always goes with the top-scoring token, so the answers are predictable. Turn it up and lower-scoring tokens get a real shot, which makes the writing more creative and also more likely to drift off track. You can drag the slider yourself in the next section.

TL;DR
  • An LLM writes by predicting one token at a time.
  • It's working from patterns in its training text, and nothing in that process checks facts.
  • Temperature controls how often it gambles on a less likely token.

    Why chatbots make things up

    When a chatbot states something false like it's a fact, that's called a hallucination. The chatbot has no idea what's true, so it isn't lying. It's doing what it always does, which is writing text that sounds likely. Usually likely and true line up. When they don't, the chatbot sounds exactly as confident as before.

    In 2023, two New York lawyers turned in a court filing that cited six cases ChatGPT had made up. The judge in Mata v. Avianca fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000.

    Where hallucinations come from

    • The topic barely showed up in the training data, like a small local event or a brand-new study, so the model fills the gaps with stuff that sounds right.
    • Training stopped at a certain date. The model won't know about anything newer unless it's hooked up to search, and even then it can mix things up.
    • It's trained to be helpful, so it tends to give you some answer instead of saying "I don't know."
    • It glues a real fact to the wrong name, date, or number, because each piece sounds fine by itself. One of the claims in the game below does exactly this.

    How to catch one

    Get suspicious when you see an exact statistic with no source, a quote you can't find anywhere else, or a citation that looks a little too perfect. To check, search the exact title or quote, find a second source that didn't come from AI, and if the chatbot says where it got something, go read that source yourself.

    Blind spots

    Most of the text these models learn from is in English and comes from the internet. They know a lot about things the internet talks about constantly and much less about a small town, a less common language, or a niche hobby. If your question is about one of those, check the answer twice as hard.
